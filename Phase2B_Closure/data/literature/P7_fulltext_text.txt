Abstract Enhancing predictive modeling accuracy in wheat ( Triticum aestivum ) breeding through the integration of high‐throughput phenotyping (HTP) data with genomic information is crucial for maximizing genetic gain. In this study, spanning four locations in the southeastern United States over 3 years, models to predict grain yield (GY) were investigated through different cross‐validation approaches. The results demonstrate the superiority of multivariate comprehensive models that incorporate both genomic and HTP data, particularly in accurately predicting GY across diverse locations and years. These HTP‐incorporating models achieve prediction accuracies ranging from 0.59 to 0.68, compared to 0.40–0.54 for genomic‐only models when tested under different prediction scenarios both across years and locations. The comprehensive models exhibit superior generalization to new environments and achieve the highest accuracy when trained on diverse datasets. Predictive accuracy improves as models incorporate data from multiple years, highlighting the importance of considering temporal dynamics in modeling approaches. The study reveals that multivariate prediction outperformed genomic prediction methods in predicting lines across years and locations. The percentage of top 25% lines selected based on multivariate prediction was higher compared to genomic‐only models, indicated by higher specificity, which is the proportion of correctly identified top‐yielding lines that matched the observed top 25% performance across different sites and years. Additionally, the study addresses the prediction of untested locations based on other locations within the same year and in new years at previously tested locations. Findings show the comprehensive models effectively extrapolate to new environments, highlighting their potential for guiding breeding strategies. Core Ideas 
 Integrating high‐throughput phenotyping (HTP) data with genomics significantly boosts wheat yield prediction accuracy. Comprehensive models achieve 0.59–0.68 accuracy, outperforming genomic‐only models. Multivariate predictions can more efficiently select top‐performing lines across years and locations. HTP and genomic data improve generalization to new environments in wheat breeding. Temporal dynamics in phenotypic traits enhance predictive power in breeding models.

INTRODUCTION Bread wheat, scientifically referred to as  Triticum aestivum , plays a vital role in ensuring worldwide food security. Approximately 95% of wheat output consists of this particular kind, which serves as a key staple in the diets of approximately 30% of the global population (Giraldo et al.,  2019 ). According to Bélanger and Pilling ( 2019 ), the global population is expected to reach about 10 billion by 2050, with a particular concentration in emerging nations and urban areas, leading to a substantial rise in the need for wheat worldwide. In order to fulfill this requirement, breeders must attain a yearly growth yield augmentation of over 1% (Peña‐Bautista et al.,  2017 ). This is a challenge for breeders to generate wheat varieties that not only have high yields in certain regions but also can endure the unpredictable conditions caused by climate change. Genomic prediction and selection (GS) have significantly transformed plant breeding tactics by providing a proactive method to predict complex traits such as grain yield (GY). This methodology employs molecular markers distributed across the entire genome, transcending the standard way of marker‐assisted selection (Bassi et al.,  2016 ). GS utilizes single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) to generate genomic estimated breeding values for different lines. While predictions can be made even when phenotypic data are limited or unavailable, accurate phenotyping is critical during the initial model training phase to ensure reliable estimates. This allows for the early identification of superior genotypes (Poland et al.,  2012 ). Genomic prediction approaches enable accurate calculation of breeding values from phenotypic records of related genotypes, even when the datasets are unbalanced. Integrating data about family members when estimating genetic merit significantly enhances the accuracy of predicting genetic merit, particularly when there are few phenotypic records for the line being predicted (Heffner et al.,  2009 ). Incorporating multi‐environment data into GS models can enhance prediction accuracy in the presence of environmental fluctuations, where the fundamental selection criteria in classical breeding approaches is prioritizing GY. Nevertheless, the existence of genotype by environment (G × E) interaction causes heterogeneity in various growth conditions, posing a challenge in using a singular value to differentiate across genotypes. This elevates the likelihood of inadvertently rejecting lines that are well‐suited for the actual target population of environments or retaining unsuitable genotypes in breeding trials. Multi‐environment GS involves analyzing performance data from multiple locations and years, allowing breeders to identify genotypes that consistently exhibit high performance across many environmental situations (Rutkoski et al.,  2016 ). Analyses that explicitly incorporate G × E interaction allow for the computation of how various genotypes function in distinct environmental conditions, offering vital insights on the stability and flexibility of genotypic performance across various locales and time periods. Multi‐environment GS models can utilize these data to anticipate the performance of genotypes in untested environmental conditions. Including the capability to estimate how genotypes will perform in future years at locations that have already been studied. This feature improves the accuracy of predictions by considering the dynamic interactions between genotypes and evolving environmental variables throughout time (Lado et al.,  2016 ). Integrating genomic and high‐throughput phenotyping (HTP) data in models that span many years and locales provides a robust vigilance against unforeseen challenges, such as crop loss caused by unfavorable weather conditions or the inability to sow due to a shortage of seeds (Montesinos‐López et al.,  2018 ). By considering the G × E interaction and utilizing data from various environments, multi‐environment GS provides breeders with a more thorough comprehension of how different genotypes perform. Enabling breeders to choose cultivars that have wide adaptability and resilience, ultimately leading to the sustainable enhancement of crop productivity (Dwivedi et al.,  2020 ). HTP data integration into GS models offers a significant breakthrough in breeding, especially when the data are acquired using aerial drone‐based spectral imaging. This approach offers a rapid and cost‐efficient method for collecting environmental and phenotypic data (Yang et al.,  2020 ). These models utilize combined genomic and phenotypic data to assess how different genotypes respond to differing environmental conditions (Crain et al.,  2018 ). Through this, the assessment of complex relationships that affect trait expression in various growing conditions by combining information on genetic composition, observable traits, and environmental influences can occur (Kaushal et al.,  2024 ). A comprehensive approach like this enables a more sophisticated comprehension of the G × E interaction, where the models can discern how certain genetic variations interact with environmental signals to regulate the expression of traits (Araus & Cairns,  2014 ). The HTP methodology facilitates the assessment of different attributes and interactions with the surrounding environments while avoiding the expenses and time constraints associated with traditional phenotyping methods. Affording researchers and wheat breeders the opportunity to efficiently gain critical knowledge about the relationship between genotype performance and environmental factors, facilitating more informed and timely breeding decisions. Core Ideas 
 Integrating high‐throughput phenotyping (HTP) data with genomics significantly boosts wheat yield prediction accuracy. Comprehensive models achieve 0.59–0.68 accuracy, outperforming genomic‐only models. Multivariate predictions can more efficiently select top‐performing lines across years and locations. HTP and genomic data improve generalization to new environments in wheat breeding. Temporal dynamics in phenotypic traits enhance predictive power in breeding models. 
 HTP allows for the possibility of finding wheat cultivars that exhibit resilience to adverse climatic circumstances or constraints related to planting. By examining the spectral fingerprints linked to stress reactions, such as drought or heat stress, breeders can pinpoint genotypes that possess exceptional resilience to these conditions. Also, through the evaluation of how particular genetic variations interact with environmental factors, these models can predict the performance of different genotypes even in new growing locations. Subsequently, this data may be utilized to generate well‐informed breeding choices, guaranteeing the selection of cultivars that will achieve maximum performance in difficult conditions. These models ensure the reliability of breeding programs, enabling informed decisions even when working with incomplete datasets. Shahi et al. ( 2022 ) showcased the efficacy of using in‐season physiological data, such as photosynthetic efficiency and drought tolerance, in predicting high‐yielding wheat varieties over many years. Their research emphasizes the importance of dynamic features in enhancing the precision of predicting wheat output in various climatic conditions. Guo et al. ( 2020 ) conducted a study that aimed to incorporate water regime and post‐anthesis heat stress responses into multivariate genomic prediction models. These authors wanted to identify and select wheat varieties that are tolerant to both drought and heat in order to show the importance of water‐use efficiency and heat stress‐related characteristics in the selection of drought‐ and heat‐tolerant wheat lines. Hence, improving the accuracy of yield predictions and enhancing the ability of crops to withstand stressful circumstances. Meanwhile, Jarquin et al. ( 2014 ) highlighted the crucial importance of incorporating the modeling of G × E interaction into genomic prediction frameworks. Their study improves the accuracy of prediction models by including G × E interaction, which takes into consideration how genotype performance varies from environment to environment. Expanding on these findings, Persa et al. ( 2021 ) conducted an in‐depth study on the advantages of evaluating multiple traits in genomic prediction by examining the significance of considering a wide range of agronomic traits to enhance the accuracy of predictions and enable the identification of superior genotypes that perform well across environments. Persa and colleagues showed that by including data on various characteristics into GS models, they were able to achieve higher prediction accuracy leading to accelerated genetic gain. Intimating how their more complex model outperformed the baseline maker matrix model in prediction, allowing them to identify genotypes with greater performance under diverse environmental conditions. The present study seeks to develop a strategy that combines genomic data and spectral data obtained from uncrewed aerial vehicles (UAVs) to predict grain production in un‐phenotyped wheat lines across various growing conditions and locations in the southeastern United States. Conducting a comparison analysis of multivariate genomic prediction and univariate genomic prediction and strategically utilizing multi‐location data, we can assess the accuracy of genomic predictions. The objective of our initiative is to utilize the combined power of genomic data and information derived from HTP platforms to enhance prediction accuracy, thus offering the potential to accelerate wheat variety development. 2. MATERIALS AND METHODS 2.1. Plant genetic material and experimental design The field experiments were conducted over a period of three consecutive growing seasons, starting from 2021 and ending in 2023. These trials were carried out at multiple locations around the southeastern United States as part of the SunGrains breeding cooperative. SunGrains is a joint research and cultivar development project that brings together the wheat and small grains breeding programs of seven universities in the southeastern region of the United States. These universities include the University of Arkansas, Clemson University, University of Florida, University of Georgia, Louisiana State University, North Carolina State University, and Texas A&M University. This collaborative endeavor facilitates the testing of breeding nurseries in multiple locations and encourages the sharing of germplasm and data among participating programs. Its goal is to improve the efficiency and productivity of each breeding program, thereby promoting the development of superior wheat varieties. The study employed soft red winter wheat elite lines derived from different breeding programs of SunGrains in the southeastern United States, as well as affiliate universities such as the University of Kentucky, Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University, and the University of Maryland. Each year, genomic data for these wheat lines is systematically gathered across several nurseries using SNP markers to characterize the lines. Data collection was conducted at experimental stations located in four different places: Citra, FL; Winnsboro, LA; Plains, GA; and Florence, SC (Table  S1 ). These locations function as communal sites for the SunGrains nursery, with the production lines spread out among them. Table  1  displays the count of lines inside each site for each year, as well as the count of lines that are shared between site‐years, where a particular site within a particular year is considered a unique environment. Not all lines are common to all sites throughout a year, and only a limited number of lines are shared from one year to the subsequent year. There were a total of 874 unique genotypes tested in the experiment, with the overlap of genotypes across environments varying from year to year; some lines were only tested in one environment, while others were present in all of the environments, with a total of 11 lines being present across all years. The wheat lines utilized in this research were cultivated in plots measuring 5.11–5.57 m 2 . The implementation of fertilizers and chemicals, as well as other management and agronomic procedures, adhered to the prescribed parameters for each location to ensure optimal development and production potential. Traits measurement Data on days to heading (DTH), GY, normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), and canopy temperature (CT) were recorded. The DTH was measured for each plot as the time elapsed from planting until the day when 50% of the plants had reached the heading stage, as determined by the Zadoks scale (Zadoks et al.,  1974 ). GY was established by using a combine plot harvester to harvest all rows. The computation is the division of the total weight of grain by the area of the plot while considering a moisture content of 13%. The resulting value is then converted to kilograms per hectare (kg ha −1 ). 2.2.1. UAV‐based high‐throughput phenotyping The aerial multispectral and thermal imaging platform comprises a DJI Matrice 300 Quadcopter drone UAV manufactured by DJI Technology Co. The platform is equipped with a two‐in‐one multispectral imaging device and a thermal imaging camera. The drone, equipped with multispectral and thermal sensors, was flown twice during the study's growing seasons at an altitude of 30 m. During each growing season, the drone was flown twice to capture multispectral and thermal data. The first flight was conducted about 5–7 days after the plants had reached heading (timepoint 1), and the second flight was scheduled 2 weeks later (timepoint 2) to monitor further development and changes in the plants. Previous research demonstrated that traits measured at specific growth stages, particularly following heading and during the early milk stage, are the most reliable predictors of GY across various environments (Babar et al.,  2006 ; Guo et al.,  2020 ). As such, the UAV flights were timed to capture data at these crucial points—once shortly after heading and again 2 weeks later. During these growth stages, traits such as NDVI and CT offer the most valuable insights into plant growth and yield potential, making them essential for accurate prediction models. The MicaSense Altum PT (AgEagle,  2024 ) was used to gather both raw multispectral and thermal imaging data, which was then processed using the Pix4D (Pix4dmapper) and QGIS ( 2018 ) imaging solutions software. Data collection flights were carried out during the period of highest daily temperatures (between 12:00 p.m. and 3:00 p.m.) under generally clear weather conditions to minimize the impact of clouds on the data. Prior to each flight, the MicaSense Altum PT sensor captured several images of the MicaSense calibrated reflectance panel. These images are utilized by imaging software during post‐processing of the multispectral and thermal images in order to adjust for the prevailing environmental lighting conditions during each flight mission. The multispectral and thermal imaging data were processed using the Pix4Dmapper software, with the data provided in the .tif (GeoTIFF) file format. Pix4D's 3‐step processing pipeline was employed to generate digital surface models (DSM), orthomosaics, reflectance maps, and spectral index maps from the raw imaging data. The process began by uploading the captured images into Pix4Dmapper, followed by radiometric calibration to standardize the pixel values across different images. The next step involved aligning the images to create a sparse point cloud. Ground control points were then added for georeferencing and to enhance accuracy. After this, a dense point cloud was generated, which formed the basis for constructing the DSM. The orthomosaic was subsequently created by projecting the calibrated images onto the DSM, resulting in a geometrically corrected image that combines all individual photos into a seamless map. Using the generated orthomosaic, the NDVI was calculated through the Index Calculator tool, facilitating pixel‐by‐pixel analysis for efficient production of index maps (Pix4D,  2011 ). NDVI is calculated using the following equation:
 ( ρ NIR − ρ red ) / ( ρ NIR + ρ red ) where  ρ red  represents the spectral reflectance of the red region of the light spectrum collected by the sensor and  ρ NIR  represents the spectral reflectance recorded from the near infrared region of the electromagnetic spectrum. Pix4D was used to build thermal orthomosaic maps for each flight done in the different fields. The thermal imaging data from the MicaSense Altum PT sensor was calibrated internally by the sensor's built‐in radiometric calibration functionality. This calibration process involves the sensor's onboard systems accounting for changes in ambient temperature, sensor temperature, and lens characteristics, ensuring that thermal pixel values are consistently converted to accurate temperature readings in degrees Celsius. The Altum PT employs radiometric thermal calibration techniques that adjust each pixel value based on predefined calibration curves, compensating for variations during data capture. The calibration allows for consistent and reliable temperature data without requiring external calibration panels or additional data loggers. By relying on the Altum PT's internal calibration system, we ensured accurate thermal measurements across the field trials, facilitating robust predictive modeling of crop traits linked to temperature. Orthomosaics depicting either NDVI or thermal heat maps of the entire field were transferred from Pix4D to QGIS. In QGIS, these orthomosaics may be divided into segments based on individual plots. The average pixel value within each plot segment was calculated using the zonal statistics plugin in QGIS. Genotyping High‐quality DNA was extracted from wheat seedling leaf tissue using the sbeadex plant maxi kit on an oKtopure automated extraction system (LGC Genomics LLC). Following the methodology published in  Poland et al. (2012) , genotyping‐by‐sequencing (GBS) libraries were generated using the restriction enzymes PstI and MspI. Post‐digestion, fragmented sample DNA was ligated to barcoded sequencing adapters. Pools containing 384 uniquely barcoded sample libraries were sequenced on Novaseq 6000 SP flowcells with a 100 base pair read length (Illumina Inc.). Sequencing reads were aligned to the International Wheat Genome Consortium (IWGSC,  2014 ) RefSeqv1.0 reference genome ( https://wheat‐urgi.versailles.inra.fr/Seq‐Repository/Assemblies ) using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner version 0.7.12 (Li et al.,  2009 ) and SNP calling was implemented by the TASSEL 5GBSv2 pipeline version 5.2.35 (Glaubitz et al.,  2014 ). Following sequencing, all genotypes underwent genetic characterization through GBS, which allowed for the identification of SNPs across the wheat genome. This was conducted using the TASSEL v. 5 software, filtering out markers with over 80% missing data and minor allele frequencies less than 0.05 as well as those with greater than 10% heterozygosity to ensure data quality. To address missing data, the Beagle version 5.2 (Browning & Browning,  2016 ) software was utilized for imputation. This algorithm leverages linkage disequilibrium information and the k‐nearest neighbor approach to impute missing genotypic data, thereby enhancing the completeness of the genomic dataset. Ultimately, the final genomic dataset for each year comprised approximately 23,000 SNP markers, providing a comprehensive genetic profile for the wheat genotypes under study. The range of markers across the chromosomes used in this study, along with their respective percentages, can be found in Table  S2 . This distribution provides an overview of the genomic coverage, which is crucial for accurate prediction modeling. The distribution reflects an adequate representation across most chromosomes. 2.4. Phenotypic data analysis Analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests were conducted for each site‐year, assuming a mixed linear model. The R package “lme4” and “emmeans” (Bates et al.,  2016 ) were utilized to estimate the best linear unbiased estimate (BLUE) for GY, NDVI, and CT by using the following equation and assuming environment and block as random effects and genotype as fixed: Y i j k ′ = μ + g i + E j + B j k + g E i j + e i j k where  Y i j k ′  represents the observed value;  μ  is the general mean;  g i  is the genotypic effect of the  i th genotypes;  E j  is the effect of environment  j ;  B j ( k )  is the block effect k in environment j;  g E i j  is the  j th genotype by  i th environment interaction effect; and  e i j k  represents the random error accounting for the non‐explained variability for the model's terms. Block effects, along with error, were assumed to follow independent normal distributions (IID). To account for the confounding influence of phenology, DTH was included as a covariate in the model when calculating BLUEs for GY, ensuring that the effect of maturity on yield‐related traits was controlled for, as previously done in Guo et al. ( 2020 ). This correction is essential because DTH is often correlated with GY, NDVI, and CT and could confound their relationship if left unaccounted for. To obtain the broad sense heritability of the traits for each environment, the variances of each effect were obtained with the following model:
 Y i j ′ = μ + g i + B k + e i k where in the model, genotype and block were considered random effects. The broad sense heritability ( H 
 2 ) for each environment was then calculated as:
 H 2 = σ G 2 σ G 2 + σ e 2 r × 100 where  σ G 2  and  σ e 2  and correspond to the variance components of the genotypic and the residual effects, and  r  is the number of blocks. The objective was to analyze separately each environment to accurately measure the genetic variation and the influence of the different environments. This approach allows for a more detailed understanding of how traits are inherited under different environmental settings. 2.5. Prediction models To improve grain production in wheat lines suited for the southern region of the United States, seven independent prediction models were developed using the model structure established by Jarquin et al. ( 2014 ) as a foundational baseline. This model employs a Bayesian approach to account for both spatial variation and G × E interactions, making it particularly well suited for multi‐environment trials. Its flexibility in capturing complex genetic and environmental interactions provides a framework for assessing trait performance across diverse conditions. In this study, the integration of this baseline structure with high‐throughput phenotypic data, such as NDVI and CT, allows for further refinement of prediction accuracy, especially under varying environmental influences. Multivariate and multi‐environment prediction approaches were utilized by these models and are listed as M1–M7 in the sections below. These methodologies include genetic, spectroscopic, and temperature data with their predictions. The BLUEs that were obtained from the model described in Section  2.4  were utilized as inputs for the prediction models that are being addressed here. 2.5.1. M1: Genotypic effects (G + E) Model M1 integrates genotypic/genomic ( g ) and environmental ( E ) effects to predict GY of different wheat lines ( i ) and environments ( j ):
 Y i j = μ + E j + g i + ε i j The model itself comprises a constant mean ( μ ), environmental effects ( E j ), genotypic effects ( g i ), and residual ( ε i j ), where  Y i j  is the BLUE for GY for the  i th genotype in the  j th environment;  E j  represents the environmental effect;  g i  represents the genomic main effect corresponding to the  i th genotype, and the vector of genomic effects  g = { g i }  follows a multivariate normal distribution such that  g  ∼  N ( 0 ,  G 
 σ g 2 ) with  G = X X ′ p  denoting the genomic relationship matrix,  X  is the centered and standardized (by columns) matrix of  p  SNP markers;  σ g 2  is the corresponding variance component; and  ε i j ∼ N ( 0 , σ ε 2 )  with  σ ε 2  the variance component of the error term. The correlation between the impacts of the random variables is determined by the off‐diagonal values of  G . Consequently, this model offers the possibility of borrowing information between different lines. This enables the ability to predict the performance of lines that have not undergone evaluation in any field trial, for instance. 2.5.2. M2: Genotypic, environment, and genotype–environment interaction effects (G + E + G × E) Model M2 extends model M1 by incorporating  G × E  interaction effects:
 Y i j = μ + E j + g i + g E i j + ε i j This model is similar to the previous model, aside from the addition of  g E i j , which denotes the interaction between the  i th genotype with the  j th environment and is assumed to have a multivariate normal distribution. Such that  g E = { g E i j } ∼ MN ( 0 , Z g G Z g T # Z E Z E T σ g E 2 )  where  Z g  and  Z E  are the incidence matrices connecting phenotypes with genotypes and environments, respectively; and  σ g E 2  is the corresponding variance component. 2.5.3. M3: HTP effects (H + E) This phenomic‐based model is similar to M1 for the prediction of GY, but rather than having SNP markers as its covariates, it relies on the incorporation of phenotypic information from the HTP data kernel ( H ). So that model M3 is represented as follows:
 Y i j = μ + E j + H i + ε i l where  H i  represents the phenotypic main effect of the  i th genotype. The joint distribution of the involved phenotypic traits can be modeled as  H = { H i } ∼ N ( 0 , P σ P 2 ) , where  P = SS ′ m  is the phenotypic trait‐derived relationship matrix with  S  as a matrix made up of the centered and standardized BLUE values of the  m  phenotypic traits and  σ P 2  denotes the corresponding variance component. 2.5.4. M4: HTP, environment, and HTP–environment interaction effects (H + E + H × E) Model M4 expands model M3 by considering the interaction between HTP and the environmental effects (H × E), allowing the impact of how HTP influences genotype responses across different environments to be gauged:
 Y i j = μ + E j + H i + H E i j + ε i j where the terms for M4 are the same as for M3, aside from the addition of  H E i j , which represents the phenotypic vector interactions between the  i th genotype and the  j th environment modeled with HTP data.  H E i j  was assumed to have a multivariate normal distribution such that  H E = { H E i j } = N ( 0 , Z g H Z g T # Z E Z E T σ H E 2 )  where  σ H E 2  represents the corresponding variance component (Jarquin et al.,  2014 ). 2.5.5. M5: Genotypic and HTP–environment interaction effects (G+ E + H × E) Models M5 and M6 attempt to examine the impact of various combinations of data inclusion, specifically the incorporation of SNP markers and HTP data, on the predicted accuracy for GY. The M5 model integrates genomic SNP markers and HTP‐linked trait data to estimate GY. This model contains the SNP marker and environment main effects as  g  and E, respectively, as well as the interaction between HTP and the environment (H  ×  E):
 Y i j = μ + E j + g i + H E i j + ε i j 2.5.6. M6: HTP and genotype–environment interaction effects (H+ E + G × E) Model M6 is somewhat an inversion of M5 in that it contains the HTP and environment main effects as H and E, respectively, and the interaction between genotype and environment:
 Y i j = μ + E j + H i + g E i j + ε i j The main difference between the M5 and M6 models is the focus that is placed on each of them. Model M5 is centered on the incorporation of genotypic, environmental, and HTP–environment interaction effects, whereas model M6 is centered on the incorporation of high‐throughput phenotyping data in addition to environmental factors and G × E interactions. 2.5.7. M7: Genotypic, HTP, G × E, and HTP–environment interaction effects (G + H+ E + G × E + H × E) The M7 model integrates all the different terms that were taken into consideration in the M1 through M6 models. It provides a complete prediction framework that considers genotypic impacts, environmental circumstances, HTP, and the interactions between these factors in order to properly predict GY across a variety of habitats in the southeastern United States.
 Y i j = μ + E j + g i + H i + H E i j + g E i j + ε i j As seen in the equation for M7, all previously described terms are contained therein, with both the HTP by environment and  G × E  interaction being present for this model. 2.6. Each technique was designed to target prediction scenarios that are pertinent to wheat breeding in the southeastern United States. The purpose of these CV schemes was to replicate real‐life situations that occur in breeding programs. Prediction of lines in incomplete trials (sparse testing)—CV2 This scenario is employed to tackle the task of predicting a specific subset of examined genotypes in a specific subset of examined environments. Some genotypes have been assessed in specific contexts, whereas their evaluation in other environments is still pending. In order to control for genomic relatedness among lines, a discriminant analysis of principal components (DAPC) was performed separately for each year, using all SNP markers for that year (Jombart et al.,  2010 ). The population was stratified into 10 clusters based on the DAPC results. From each cluster, an appropriate number of lines were randomly selected to form five CV subgroups, ensuring that the relatedness within clusters was controlled across validation and training sets. In this way, no lines from the same DAPC cluster were present in both the training and validation sets at the same time. This CV process was replicated 10 times for model training. Within each fold, 20% of the phenotypic records were used for prediction, while 80% were used for training. The means (and standard errors) across replicates were then computed for each environment. 2.6.2. Predicting new lines (not observed in any environment yet)—CV1 CV1 is employed to predict genotypes that have not been observed in any tested environment. Here, certain individuals were not observed in any tested environment, while other lines were tested in these environments. The goal is to assess the models' ability to extrapolate to new genotypes based on patterns learned from observed data. The CV1 analysis also employs a fivefold CV scheme with an 80/20 split using the results of the DAPC. In this scenario, no data from masked lines were considered in any of the environments such that genotypes were partitioned into folds rather than phenotypes, ensuring that all phenotypic records from the same genotype are grouped together within the same fold. Approximately 20% of the genotypes are designated as the validation or testing set, while the remaining majority (around 80% of the genotypes) were utilized for model calibration. Like CV2, each fold was sequentially predicted using the other folds as the training data, and this process was repeated 10 times. Here, the genomic similarities between the lines in the training and testing sets play a major role in the performance of the models. 2.6.3. Predicting lines in untested sites—CV0‐S The CV0‐S cross‐validation focuses on predicting the performance of genotypes in untested sites. This scenario entails predicting the performance of genotypes in sites that were not previously utilized for training purposes. Genotypes that have been seen in specific situations are now being extrapolated to predict their performance in untested sites. This approach offers valuable insights into the extent to which the models can apply to a wide range of environmental situations. This approach is mostly employed when there are no phenotypic records available for any of the observed lines in the target site, with the overall effectiveness of this predictive strategy relying on whether the conditions in the unobserved sites are within the range of conditions in those used for testing, as well as the performance and quantity of phenotypic records from the same genotypes observed in other sites within the test set. In this context, the predictions are generated by excluding one site and utilizing those remaining as the training set. Subsequently, the correlations between the predicted and actual values for each environment are calculated. This implies a poststratification of the predicted values for one site to each year. The CV0‐based scenarios, unlike other CV scenarios, do not utilize random division of data. Instead, it is executed or carried out only once. This implies that the dataset is divided into separate sets for training and testing, and this division remains constant throughout the assessment process. 2.6.4. Predicting lines in untested years—CV0‐Y The CV0‐Y framework utilizes an approach akin to forward prediction to not only anticipate the performance of genotypes in untested situations but to assess them by comparing predicted values to observed values in subsequent years. This method involves using data from prior years, such as phenotypic data (e.g., GY and biomass) and genotypic data (e.g., SNP markers), as the training set to predict the performance of genotypes in future years. The performance in succeeding years is then used as the testing set to evaluate the accuracy of the predictions. For example, historical GY, genomic, and secondary indicator trait data like NDVI and CT from prior years, such as 2019, is used as a reference point for estimating the performance in the subsequent year, 2020. Similarly, the training population (TP) for estimating the performance in the upcoming year, 2022 (VP), is formed by combining data from numerous prior years, such as 2020 and 2021. The model strategically captures environmental effects primarily at the location level, ensuring that environmental factors are accounted for based on spatial variability between different sites. When predicting the performance of genotypes in a new year, the environmental effects are applied at the location level. This emphasizes the significant impact of geographical environments on genotype performance, highlighting how differences between locations contribute uniquely to the observed phenotypic traits. By using this advanced forward CV prediction technique, the study obtains a more accurate comprehension of the models' ability to handle future, unseen years. This, in turn, improves the evaluation of genomic selection effectiveness within the breeding program context. 2.7. Model evaluation The efficacy of the implemented prediction models was evaluated by calculating the within‐environment correlations between the predicted and observed values. This provided a reliable measure of how well the model performed at the environment level. Due to the variable environmental circumstances and varying sample sizes, it was crucial to conduct a thorough evaluation of predictability across different contexts. To achieve this, a weighted average correlation was computed across environments, following the methodology proposed by Tiezzi et al. ( 2017 ) and utilized by Canella Vieira et al. ( 2022 ). The computation of the weighted average correlation involved calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient between the predicted and observed records within each environment. The sampling variance and the number of observations for each environment were considered in the aggregation of correlations across environments as follows  r φ = ∑ l = 1 11 r j V ( r j ) ∑ l = 1 11 1 V ( r j )  with  V ( r j ) = 1 − r j 2 n j − 2  and  n j  is the number of genotypes at the  j th environment. This approach accounts for both the uncertainty associated with each environment and the variability in sample sizes across environments. The weighted average correlation metric offers a nuanced understanding of the model's predictive performance. 3. RESULTS 3.1. DISCUSSION Integrating HTP and genomic data in predictive modeling is a smart method to utilize many sources of information for improving the precision and effectiveness of crop breeding operations. The rationale for this integration is based on the supplemental features of genomic and phenomic datasets, which encompass distinct facets of the genotype‐phenotype correlation. Genomic data offer valuable insights into the fundamental genetic structure of characteristics, giving breeders the ability to pinpoint genomic areas linked to favorable phenotypic traits (Jarquin et al.,  2017 ). HTP data, including multispectral and thermal imaging, provide noninvasive and quick assessments of plant characteristics associated with physiological processes, such as CT and NDVI (Sandhu et al.,  2021 ). Spectral imaging analyzes the quantity of light reflected at different wavelengths, providing information regarding plant health, biomass, and stress levels. Thermal imaging is a technique that detects infrared radiation generated by plants, providing useful information on temperature changes associated with physiological responses. This helps to identify how various genotypes behave in different environmental situations (Jackson et al.,  2023 ). Studies have also demonstrated that multivariate modeling techniques, which may incorporate a variety of genetic and phenotypic components, are frequently superior to univariate models in capturing the interplay between multiple traits. Research conducted by Ibba et al. ( 2020 ) showed that using multivariate genomic predictions and multi‐environment trials can enhance breeding outcomes under a range of scenarios. The current study expands these findings by investigating the use of HTP and genetic data integration in agronomic crop breeding programs, like wheat. This was carried out with the specific purpose of predicting GY across various locations and years. According to Guo et al. ( 2020 ), the variations in average GY across locations and years demonstrate the influence of environmental factors and gene–environment interactions on wheat productivity. These fluctuations in GY highlight the dynamic nature of crop growth systems and emphasize the need for approaches that effectively account for G × E interactions to improve yield predictions. In this research, GY, NDVI, and CT exhibited varying  H 
 2  values in different sites and years, shedding light on the genetic control and sensitivity to environmental influences of these traits. Wheat genotype performance in the research areas may have been influenced by unique environmental factors found only in the southeastern United States, such as soil composition, temperature changes, and disease pressure. An important aspect of the approach was controlling for the genetic relatedness among lines through population stratification, achieved via the DAPC to cluster the genotypes into groups based on their genetic similarity. This stratification ensured that lines within the same group were not present in both the validation and training sets simultaneously. By controlling for relatedness in this way, the risk of overestimating prediction accuracies due to shared genetic background between the training and validation sets is minimized. Without such stratification, the models could be biased, predicting well simply because the training and validation sets would share closely related genotypes, rather than capturing true predictive power across a diverse population. By stratifying based on genetic clusters, the models are made more applicable to real‐world breeding scenarios, where genetic diversity must be accounted for to ensure accurate and generalizable predictions. The first scenario tested used the CV2 scheme to assess incomplete trials. In this approach, including HTP data with genomic marker information gave way to significant prediction accuracy improvements for different models, elucidating the complementary nature of these two data types. Shahi et al. ( 2022 ) previously established that multivariate models that include genomic and HTP data regularly perform better than univariate models in predicting complex traits like GY. Scenario 2, which used the CV1 approach to predict new lines, saw similar patterns of prediction accuracies going up when a secondary data source (HTP) is included along with genomic data. The differences in how well the two scenarios did can largely be attributed to the different way in which the data are masked. In CV2, there were incomplete trials where just a subset of genotypes was concealed. For CV1, wholly new lines that were not detected in any tested environment were masked. The differences observed between scenario 1 (CV1) and scenario 2 (CV2) highlight not only how these CV approaches function but also how they impact model performance. In CV2, where only a subset of genotypes is masked, the model leverages prior observations for many genotypes, leading to generally higher prediction accuracies. This is because the model has more complete information from other environments and genotypes. In contrast, CV1 involves masking completely new lines that may not have been tested in any environment, making predictions more difficult due to the lack of direct data on these genotypes. Prediction accuracies tend to be lower in CV1, a trend reflected in our results, which show higher accuracies for CV2. These differences underscore the necessity for customized strategies in genomic selection. Different CV schemes present unique challenges, and tailored approaches are needed to maximize predictive accuracy depending on the breeding goals and experimental designs. For scenario 3, where new sites were predicted with the CV0‐S approach, certain models showed promise when predicting missing location data based on information from other locations. Suggesting that breeders can leverage relatively inexpensive HTP techniques like drone‐based multispectral and thermal cameras to capture valuable phenotypic information and improve prediction precision within a short time and with reduced labor costs. HTP methods enable the capture of spatial variability in environmental factors (e.g., temperature, canopy coverage, or NDVI) across entire fields or trials, adding valuable data that can fill gaps in phenotypic measurements. Beyond this, the predictive models were able to leverage genomic information through the concept of borrowing information, whereby genetic relationships between different genotypes allow the model to infer performance even for untested genotypes. This process is further enhanced by incorporating G × E interactions, where the models take into account how the same genotypes respond differently across various environmental conditions. By combining the genomic relationship matrix ( G ), which encodes genetic similarities, and the high‐dimensional HTP data kernel (H), which captures phenotypic traits, the models can also borrow phenotypic information across genotypes and environments. This enables predictions to be made for genotypes that may not have been tested in certain environments, using data from related genotypes that have been evaluated. Furthermore, by including multiple environments (locations and years), the models can anticipate how genotypes will perform in new or untested environments, effectively utilizing the shared genetic and phenotypic patterns observed across the dataset. Despite the trend seen in the results of increasing prediction accuracies with the inclusion of HTP data, there still is room for improvement when predicting complex quantitative traits like GY. The highest performing models under scenario 3 selected 44%–59% of the top 25% best performing lines, which may potentially be even further improved through the finer characterization of each site‐year. In a real‐world scenario, breeders may encounter the CV0‐S scheme when expanding breeding trials to new geographical locations or conditions, where historical site data may not be available, necessitating the development of generalizable predictive models to guide breeding decisions. Also, the CV0‐S scheme could be applied in plant breeding to assess genotype performance in scenarios where the number of testing sites is low or where replication is limited. Despite accuracies typically increasing from M1 to M7, the changes seen in the top 25% specificity did not particularly improve correspondingly. This inconsistency might be due to the distinction between prediction accuracy and classification accuracy; M7 utilizes HTP from multiple sources, leading to enhanced predictions in different situations. Despite this, it can rank the highest‐performing lines, although this may be affected by ambient noise or the intricacies of G × E interactions, which may have the potential to blur the distinction between the top‐performing lines and those with middling performance. Consequently, while M7 enhances overall predictive skills, its efficacy in pinpointing the highest performers may be limited in some scenarios, suggesting that further refinement or more supplementary data might increase classification accuracy. For the fourth and final scenario, new years were predicted with the CV0‐Y approach to test the potential of the model to improve prediction accuracies from one year to the next. This scenario can be more challenging than the others because it involves the forward prediction of lines based on historical data instead of predicting within existing datasets. Our results would suggest that models can still do well despite these challenges when integrating diverse data sources like NDVI and CT into a more traditional GBLUP framework. The exceptional performance of our models when compared to prior research underscores the potential of the methodology depicted in this paper to improve upon wheat breeding endeavors. Even in populations where phenology is not strongly correlated with yield, it is expected that the best models would continue to perform effectively, though the importance of individual predictors may shift. In such cases, traits like NDVI and CT may play a more significant role in driving yield predictions. The flexibility of the models, which integrate both genomic and phenotypic data, allows them to capture complex interactions, irrespective of the relative influence of phenological traits. However, the specific contribution of phenology to the accuracy of predictions may vary, and future studies could explore how a reduced correlation between phenology and yield might affect model performance. Comparative analyses showed that including NDVI and CT data from multiple timepoints consistently improved model performance, demonstrating the added value of incorporating various types of HTP data. Models that also integrated the HTP data from both collection timepoints had better accuracies than those that relied on data from a single point data collection. Showing the significance of collecting data multiple times throughout the growing season to better capture the temporal dynamics of the traits. Data taken at timepoint 2 appear to have greater predictive ability than timepoint 1. This could be due to the increased differentiation among lines as they mature, allowing for more distinct phenotypic expression later in the season. However, early‐season data (timepoint 1) provide critical baseline information that could still support initial yield predictions if incorporated effectively. Through the incorporation of HTP data taken from across the relevant growth stages, we can better capture the spectrum of plant responses to changes in the environment and thus create more accurate genomic prediction models. These findings are consistent with insights from existing literature, which emphasize the importance of integrating diverse phenotypic traits to improve predictive modeling accuracy in wheat breeding programs (Silva‐Perez et al.,  2018 ). 5. CONCLUSION The current study attempts to highlight the usage of HTP data into more established genomic prediction methods in order to improve accuracies. Through assessing a number of prediction models under four distinct CV approaches, we observed consistent increases in the model's predictive accuracy when including HTP data in the form of NDVI and CT, taken at two separate timepoints throughout the grain‐filling period. Previous research confirms the trends seen in our results (Shahi et al.,  2022 ) and together highlights the need to consider the changes over time in physical characteristics of plants to fully understand their reactions to environmental factors. Multivariate models integrating HTP data were found to outperform the univariate models that rely only on genomic data for their training. The research also highlights how the inclusion of HTP data can improve accuracies in multi‐environment prediction scenarios. This is a significant contribution to understanding how to effectively incorporate HTP data into predictive modeling across various real‐life field conditions. The overarching goal of this research was to provide effective methods to streamline the breeding process by targeting elite lines, with a focus on integrating UAV‐derived data into predictive analytics, an approach that remains contemporary in plant breeding strategies. While this study demonstrates the power of integrating HTP and genomic data for predicting GY in wheat, future research should focus on several key areas to build on these findings. Incorporating early‐season phenotypic data may serve as a valuable complement to late‐season data by initiating the prediction process earlier, potentially reducing the need for multiple in‐season measurements. This approach could enhance predictive accuracy throughout the season by combining early and late phenotypic signals. Expanding the application of these predictive models across more diverse environmental conditions and crop types would further assess the models' generalizability across breeding programs.
